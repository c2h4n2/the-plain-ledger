The Real Cost of Minimum Payments

Minimum payments are designed to keep an account in good standing, not to pay it off in any reasonable timeframe. Credit card issuers typically calculate the minimum as a small percentage of the balance — often 1-3% — plus interest and fees. That structure is the reason minimum-only payoffs can drag on for over a decade.

A worked example

Take a $5,000 balance at 22% APR, with a minimum payment set at 2% of the balance (a fairly typical structure):

  • Paying only the minimum each month, the balance takes roughly 20+ years to clear
  • Total interest paid over that time can exceed the original balance itself
  • The minimum payment amount shrinks each month as the balance drops, which is exactly why progress feels so slow — you're paying less over time even as the payoff date barely moves

Compare that to a fixed $200/month payment on the same balance: the payoff timeline drops to a few years, and total interest paid falls dramatically, because a fixed payment doesn't shrink as the balance does.

Why the minimum keeps shrinking

Most minimum payment formulas are a percentage of the current balance, not a fixed dollar amount. As the balance decreases, so does the minimum — which means less and less of each payment goes toward principal over time if you never increase the payment yourself.

The fix is simpler than it sounds

Pick a fixed payment amount above the minimum and keep paying that same fixed amount every month, even as the required minimum drops. You don't need a complicated plan — just resist the temptation to reduce your payment down to whatever the statement lists as "due."

The minimum payment is the card issuer's number. Your payoff plan should run on your own number instead.
